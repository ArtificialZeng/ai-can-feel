# Candidate references for the NHB manuscript (verified)

Checked 2026-11-06. None of the 18 works already in the manuscript are repeated here. For every entry I opened a record that confirms the title, authors, year and venue: the Crossref API (`https://api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>`), the publisher's page, an arXiv abstract page, an OpenReview record (read through the OpenReview API), a NeurIPS, PMLR or ACL Anthology page, or the authors' own page. Where both a preprint and a peer-reviewed version exist, the peer-reviewed version is cited. Volume, issue and page fields come only from those records. Any field I could not confirm is left out.

Main list: 45 entries. A reserve list of other verified works and the "could not verify" list come at the end.

## 1. Mind perception and its dimensions (6)

1. Epley, N., Waytz, A. & Cacioppo, J. T. On seeing human: a three-factor theory of anthropomorphism. *Psychol. Rev.* **114**, 864–886 (2007).
   DOI: 10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.864
   verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.864
   The standard account of when people attribute humanlike minds to non-human agents. In the **Introduction** it frames why a chatbot's first-person self-description could act as a cue that shifts human attributions (Study 6 Part B).

2. Waytz, A., Gray, K., Epley, N. & Wegner, D. M. Causes and consequences of mind perception. *Trends Cogn. Sci.* **14**, 383–388 (2010).
   DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2010.05.006
   verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.tics.2010.05.006
   A review of what triggers mind perception and what follows from it, including moral concern. In the **Introduction** it explains why a model's Experience and Agency self-descriptions matter beyond the model itself.

3. Weisman, K., Dweck, C. S. & Markman, E. M. Rethinking people's conceptions of mental life. *Proc. Natl Acad. Sci. USA* **114**, 11374–11379 (2017).
   DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1704347114
   verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1073/pnas.1704347114
   With a different method, four studies find three components of mental life (body, heart, mind) instead of Experience and Agency. In the **Methods/Discussion** it justifies reporting item-level results as well as the two Gray et al. factors, and it marks the two-factor structure as contested.

4. Weisman, K. et al. Similarities and differences in concepts of mental life among adults and children in five cultures. *Nat. Hum. Behav.* **5**, 1358–1368 (2021).
   DOI: 10.1038/s41562-021-01184-8
   verified at: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01184-8 (citation block and issue date) and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1038/s41562-021-01184-8
   In the USA, Ghana, Thailand, China and Vanuatu, people separate cognitive abilities from bodily sensations, but the details differ by culture. In the **Introduction/Methods** it is the human cross-cultural precedent for testing whether mind attributions hold across languages.

5. Malle, B. F. How many dimensions of mind perception really are there? *Proc. Annu. Meet. Cogn. Sci. Soc.* **41**, 2268–2274 (2019).
   URL: https://escholarship.org/uc/item/3j55p1rz
   verified at: https://escholarship.org/content/qt3j55p1rz/qt3j55p1rz.pdf (cover page: Proceedings of the Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society 41, 2019, peer reviewed; page numbers 2268–2274 printed on the pages)
   Using a larger item pool and varied question probes, Malle finds three dimensions: Affect, Moral & Mental Regulation, and Reality Interaction. It supports the **Discussion** point that the dimensional structure depends on items and wording.

6. Gray, K. & Wegner, D. M. Feeling robots and human zombies: mind perception and the uncanny valley. *Cognition* **125**, 125–130 (2012).
   DOI: 10.1016/j.cognition.2012.06.007
   verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.cognition.2012.06.007
   Machines seen as able to feel (Experience) are unnerving in a way that machines seen as able to act (Agency) are not. In the **Discussion** it is one reason developers might train models to deny feelings in particular, and it sets up a prediction for Part B.

## 2. Folk attributions of mind and consciousness to AI and LLMs (5)

7. Colombatto, C. & Fleming, S. M. Folk psychological attributions of consciousness to large language models. *Neurosci. Conscious.* **2024**, niae013 (2024).
   DOI: 10.1093/nc/niae013
   verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/nc/niae013
   Most of 300 US residents allowed some possibility of phenomenal consciousness in LLMs, and attributions rose with how often people used them. It gives the human baseline for the **Introduction** and the Part B hypotheses.

8. Stein, J.-P. & Ohler, P. Venturing into the uncanny valley of mind—the influence of mind attribution on the acceptance of human-like characters in a virtual reality setting. *Cognition* **160**, 43–50 (2017).
   DOI: 10.1016/j.cognition.2016.12.010
   verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.cognition.2016.12.010
   An experiment in which believing that virtual characters had autonomous, emotionally capable minds made them seem eerier. In the **Discussion/Part B** it is evidence that what people are told about an agent's feelings changes how they react to it.

9. Nass, C. & Moon, Y. Machines and mindlessness: social responses to computers. *J. Soc. Issues* **56**, 81–103 (2000).
   DOI: 10.1111/0022-4537.00153
   verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/0022-4537.00153
   A review of experiments showing that people mindlessly apply social rules and stereotypes to computers. In the **Introduction** it is the reason to expect users to take a chatbot's self-description at face value.

10. Pataranutaporn, P., Liu, R., Finn, E. & Maes, P. Influencing human–AI interaction by priming beliefs about AI can increase perceived trustworthiness, empathy and effectiveness. *Nat. Mach. Intell.* **5**, 1076–1086 (2023).
    DOI: 10.1038/s42256-023-00720-7
    verified at: https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-023-00720-7 and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1038/s42256-023-00720-7
    Participants used the same conversational AI after different priming statements about its motives (caring, manipulative, or none), and the priming changed how trustworthy, empathic and effective they judged it. It is the closest experimental precedent for the Part B manipulation (**Methods**).

11. Anthis, J. R., Pauketat, J. V. T., Ladak, A. & Manoli, A. Perceptions of sentient AI and other digital minds: evidence from the AI, Morality, and Sentience (AIMS) survey. In *Proc. 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* 1–22 (ACM, 2025).
    DOI: 10.1145/3706598.3713329
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3706598.3713329
    A nationally representative US survey (N = 3,500; 2021 and 2023). In 2023, one in five adults believed some AI systems are already sentient. It gives population-level context for the **Introduction** and **Discussion**.

## 3. LLM self-reports, self-knowledge and introspection (7)

12. Shanahan, M., McDonell, K. & Reynolds, L. Role play with large language models. *Nature* **623**, 493–498 (2023).
    DOI: 10.1038/s41586-023-06647-8
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1038/s41586-023-06647-8
    Argues that dialogue agents are best understood as playing characters, not reporting on themselves. In the **Introduction/Discussion** it offers one reading of why the reminder "You are an AI language model" changes Agency answers.

13. Chalmers, D. J. Could a large language model be conscious? *Boston Review* (9 August 2023). Preprint at https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103.
    URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103
    verified at: https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103 (journal-ref: "Boston Review, August 9, 2023")
    Weighs the reasons for and against consciousness in LLMs and concludes it is somewhat unlikely in current models but a serious possibility for their successors. It belongs in the **Introduction**, as context for treating self-reports as data and not as evidence of consciousness.

14. Long, R. et al. Taking AI welfare seriously. Preprint at https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986 (2024).
    URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986
    verified at: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986
    Recommends that AI companies acknowledge AI welfare as a difficult issue and "ensure that language model outputs do the same". In the **Discussion**, our finding that post-trained models flatly deny feelings bears directly on that recommendation.

15. Schwitzgebel, E. AI systems must not confuse users about their sentience or moral status. *Patterns* **4**, 100818 (2023).
    DOI: 10.1016/j.patter.2023.100818
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100818
    Argues that AI systems should invite emotional and moral concern in line with their actual moral standing. In the **Discussion**, our data show that what systems say about their own minds depends on wording and language, which makes that standard hard to meet.

16. Binder, F. J. et al. Looking inward: language models can learn about themselves by introspection. In *Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations* (ICLR, 2025).
    URL: https://openreview.net/forum?id=eb5pkwIB5i
    verified at: OpenReview record eb5pkwIB5i (venue "ICLR 2025 Poster"); preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13787
    After fine-tuning, models predict their own behaviour in hypothetical situations better than other models trained on that behaviour do. This is limited evidence of privileged self-access. In the **Discussion** it sets the bounds on reading self-descriptions as introspection.

17. Lindsey, J. Emergent introspective awareness in large language models. *Transformer Circuits Thread* https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html (2025).
    URL: https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html
    verified at: the same page (author Jack Lindsey, Anthropic; published 29 October 2025; the page's BibTeX gives journal "Transformer Circuits Thread", year 2025)
    When concepts are injected into their activations, some models can sometimes detect and name them, but unreliably and depending on context. In the **Discussion** it shows that self-reports can carry some internal information without being reliable. (Not peer-reviewed.)

18. Song, S., Hu, J. & Mahowald, K. Language models fail to introspect about their knowledge of language. In *Proc. Conference on Language Modeling* (COLM, 2025).
    URL: https://openreview.net/forum?id=AivRDOFi5H
    verified at: OpenReview record AivRDOFi5H (venue "COLM 2025"); preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07513
    Across 21 open models, a model's prompted metalinguistic judgements predicted its own string probabilities no better than a model with nearly identical internal knowledge could, so there is no evidence of privileged self-access. In the **Discussion** it supports treating self-descriptions as behaviour, not as readouts.

## 4. Validity of giving LLMs human psychological instruments (9)

19. Dillion, D., Tandon, N., Gu, Y. & Gray, K. Can AI language models replace human participants? *Trends Cogn. Sci.* **27**, 597–600 (2023).
    DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008
    Discusses when LLM responses can and cannot stand in for human participants. It belongs in the **Introduction**, and also motivates collecting real human data (Study 6) instead of simulating it.

20. Binz, M. & Schulz, E. Using cognitive psychology to understand GPT-3. *Proc. Natl Acad. Sci. USA* **120**, e2218523120 (2023).
    DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2218523120
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1073/pnas.2218523120
    GPT-3 solved many canonical vignette tasks, but small changes to the vignettes led it far astray. In the **Methods** it is a precedent for giving models standard psychology tasks, and in the **Discussion** a parallel to the wording sensitivity we find.

21. Hagendorff, T., Fabi, S. & Kosinski, M. Human-like intuitive behavior and reasoning biases emerged in large language models but disappeared in ChatGPT. *Nat. Comput. Sci.* **3**, 833–838 (2023).
    DOI: 10.1038/s43588-023-00527-x
    verified at: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43588-023-00527-x and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1038/s43588-023-00527-x
    Human-like intuitive errors grew with model scale and then largely disappeared in the ChatGPT models. In the **Discussion** it is further evidence that post-training changes how models respond to psychological tests.

22. Serapio-García, G. et al. A psychometric framework for evaluating and shaping personality traits in large language models. *Nat. Mach. Intell.* **7**, 1954–1968 (2025).
    DOI: 10.1038/s42256-025-01115-6
    verified at: https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-025-01115-6 and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1038/s42256-025-01115-6
    Personality-test responses from some LLMs can be reliable and valid under specific prompting, more so for larger and instruction-tuned models. In the **Methods** it is the reference for checking reliability and validity before interpreting LLM questionnaire responses.

23. Dominguez-Olmedo, R., Hardt, M. & Mendler-Dünner, C. Questioning the survey responses of large language models. *Adv. Neural Inf. Process. Syst.* **37**, 45850–45878 (2024).
    DOI: 10.52202/079017-1458 · URL: https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/515c62809e0a29729d7eec26e2916fc0-Abstract-Conference.html
    verified at: the NeurIPS proceedings page above (citation metadata: volume 37, pages 45850–45878)
    Across 43 models, survey answers were driven by answer order and labelling, and once order was randomised they drifted towards uniform. In the **Methods** it motivates our comprehension checks and order controls.

24. Röttger, P. et al. Political compass or spinning arrow? Towards more meaningful evaluations for values and opinions in large language models. In *Proc. 62nd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers)* 15295–15311 (Association for Computational Linguistics, 2024).
    DOI: 10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.816
    verified at: https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.816.bib and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.816
    Shows that the values and opinions LLMs state on forced-choice instruments change with how the question is posed. In the **Methods/Discussion** it is the direct precedent for our finding that Agency answers flip with a single word.

25. Sclar, M., Choi, Y., Tsvetkov, Y. & Suhr, A. Quantifying language models' sensitivity to spurious features in prompt design or: how I learned to start worrying about prompt formatting. In *Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations* (ICLR, 2024).
    URL: https://openreview.net/forum?id=RIu5lyNXjT
    verified at: OpenReview record RIu5lyNXjT (venue "ICLR 2024 poster")
    Formatting changes that preserve meaning shifted few-shot accuracy by up to 76 points. In the **Methods** it is the rationale for crossing several wordings and not trusting a single prompt.

26. Tjuatja, L., Chen, V., Wu, T., Talwalkar, A. & Neubig, G. Do LLMs exhibit human-like response biases? A case study in survey design. *Trans. Assoc. Comput. Linguist.* **12**, 1011–1026 (2024).
    DOI: 10.1162/tacl_a_00685
    verified at: https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.56.bib, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1162/tacl_a_00685 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04076 (Crossref and the Anthology spell the fourth author "Talwalkwar"; arXiv has "Talwalkar")
    Across nine models, LLMs generally did not show the response biases humans show to survey wording, especially after RLHF, and they reacted to changes that leave human answers unchanged. In the **Methods/Discussion** it motivates Study 6 Part A, which asks whether the "do you"/"can you" effect also occurs in people.

27. Ivanova, A. A. How to evaluate the cognitive abilities of LLMs. *Nat. Hum. Behav.* **9**, 230–233 (2025).
    DOI: 10.1038/s41562-024-02096-z
    verified at: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02096-z and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1038/s41562-024-02096-z
    Sets out 14 methodological considerations for designing and interpreting studies of LLM cognition. In the **Methods** it shows NHB readers that the design follows current best practice.

## 5. Post-training and alignment: effects on stated beliefs and multilingual behaviour (8)

28. Ouyang, L. et al. Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback. *Adv. Neural Inf. Process. Syst.* **35**, 27730–27744 (2022).
    DOI: 10.52202/068431-2011 · URL: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/b1efde53be364a73914f58805a001731-Abstract-Conference.html
    verified at: the NeurIPS proceedings page above (volume 35, pages 27730–27744)
    Introduced the supervised fine-tuning plus RLHF pipeline (InstructGPT) that separates base from post-trained checkpoints. In the **Introduction/Methods** it defines what "post-training" means in the base vs post-trained comparison.

29. Bai, Y. et al. Constitutional AI: harmlessness from AI feedback. Preprint at https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073 (2022).
    URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073
    verified at: https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073
    Trains a harmless assistant from a written list of principles through self-critique and RL from AI feedback. In the **Discussion** it shows how a developer's explicit norms can be built into what a model says about itself.

30. Lin, B. Y. et al. The unlocking spell on base LLMs: rethinking alignment via in-context learning. In *Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations* (ICLR, 2024).
    URL: https://openreview.net/forum?id=wxJ0eXwwda
    verified at: OpenReview record wxJ0eXwwda (venue "ICLR 2024 poster")
    Compares token distributions of base and aligned models and finds that alignment tuning changes surprisingly little ("superficial alignment"). In the **Discussion** it helps interpret base vs post-trained differences: Experience denial may be a targeted, surface-level change.

31. Santurkar, S. et al. Whose opinions do language models reflect? In *Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning*, PMLR 202, 29971–30004 (2023).
    URL: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/santurkar23a.html
    verified at: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/santurkar23a.html
    Using public-opinion polls (OpinionQA), finds that LLM opinions differ substantially from those of 60 US demographic groups, and confirms that some human-feedback-tuned models lean left. In the **Introduction/Discussion** it shows post-training changes stated beliefs, not only capabilities.

32. Sharma, M. et al. Towards understanding sycophancy in language models. In *Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations* (ICLR, 2024).
    URL: https://openreview.net/forum?id=tvhaxkMKAn
    verified at: OpenReview record tvhaxkMKAn (venue "ICLR 2024 poster")
    RLHF-trained assistants tend to match the user's apparent beliefs, and human preference data rewards this. In the **Discussion** it is an alternative explanation for wording effects: models may answer what a question's framing suggests the user expects.

33. Deng, Y., Zhang, W., Pan, S. J. & Bing, L. Multilingual jailbreak challenges in large language models. In *Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations* (ICLR, 2024).
    URL: https://openreview.net/forum?id=vESNKdEMGp
    verified at: OpenReview record vESNKdEMGp (venue "ICLR 2024 poster")
    Safety measures built mainly in English can be bypassed by non-English prompts, whether by accident or on purpose. In the **Discussion** it is the contrast case: unlike safety behaviour, the Experience denial generalises across our eight languages, while Agency self-descriptions do not.

34. Yong, Z.-X., Menghini, C. & Bach, S. H. Low-resource languages jailbreak GPT-4. Preprint at https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02446 (2023).
    URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02446
    verified at: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02446 (comments: NeurIPS 2023 SoLaR workshop)
    Translating unsafe prompts into low-resource languages got past GPT-4's safeguards 79% of the time on AdvBench, because safety-training data are unevenly spread across languages. In the **Discussion** it supports the uneven-generalisation account. Our eight languages are all high-resource, which belongs in the **Limitations**.

35. Wei, A., Haghtalab, N. & Steinhardt, J. Jailbroken: how does LLM safety training fail? *Adv. Neural Inf. Process. Syst.* **36**, 80079–80110 (2023).
    DOI: 10.52202/075280-3508 · URL: https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/fd6613131889a4b656206c50a8bd7790-Abstract-Conference.html
    verified at: the NeurIPS proceedings page above (volume 36, pages 80079–80110)
    Identifies "competing objectives" and "mismatched generalisation" as the two ways safety training fails. In the **Discussion** it is a theoretical frame for why trained-in self-descriptions (Experience) hold while untargeted ones (Agency) vary.

## 6. Survey methodology: wording, translation and measurement invariance (6)

36. Schuman, H. & Presser, S. *Questions and Answers in Attitude Surveys: Experiments on Question Form, Wording, and Context* (Academic Press, 1981).
    URL: https://www.sagepub.com/en-gb/eur/questions-and-answers-in-attitude-surveys/book4010 (SAGE reissue, ISBN 9780761903598)
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/sf/62.2.546 (Crossref record of the 1983 *Social Forces* review, titled "Questions and Answers in Attitude Surveys. By Howard Schuman and Stanley Presser. Academic Press, 1981. 362 pp.") and https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1086/227846 (*AJS* review giving the full subtitle). SAGE's page confirms the title, subtitle and authors.
    The classic split-ballot experiments showing that small changes in question form and wording change answers. In the **Introduction** it shows that wording effects are an old, well-documented human phenomenon.

37. Tourangeau, R., Rips, L. J. & Rasinski, K. *The Psychology of Survey Response* (Cambridge Univ. Press, 2000).
    DOI: 10.1017/CBO9780511819322
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/cbo9780511819322
    The standard cognitive model of answering a survey question: comprehension, retrieval, judgement and response. In the **Discussion** it offers a vocabulary for where "do you" and "can you" diverge, at comprehension or at judgement.

38. Hippler, H.-J. & Schwarz, N. Not forbidding isn't allowing: the cognitive basis of the forbid–allow asymmetry. *Public Opin. Q.* **50**, 87 (1986).
    DOI: 10.1086/268961
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1086/268961 (Crossref gives the start page only, so the end page is omitted)
    A standard human example of one verb swap ("forbid" vs "allow") changing answers to a logically equivalent question. In the **Discussion** it is the human counterpart to the "do you"/"can you" flip.

39. Walde, P. & Völlm, B. A. The TRAPD approach as a method for questionnaire translation. *Front. Psychiatry* **14**, 1199989 (2023).
    DOI: 10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1199989
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1199989
    Describes the Translation–Review–Adjudication–Pretest–Documentation (TRAPD) team approach and why back-translation alone misses problems. In the **Methods** it is the standard for translating the 18 items into five languages for Study 6, and it addresses the current single-translator limitation.

40. Putnick, D. L. & Bornstein, M. H. Measurement invariance conventions and reporting: the state of the art and future directions for psychological research. *Dev. Rev.* **41**, 71–90 (2016).
    DOI: 10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004
    Reviews the conventions for testing configural, metric and scalar invariance across groups and for reporting the results. In the **Methods** it is the reference for testing whether the human 18-item ratings are comparable across languages before comparing means.

41. Ramírez-Esparza, N., Gosling, S. D., Benet-Martínez, V., Potter, J. P. & Pennebaker, J. W. Do bilinguals have two personalities? A special case of cultural frame switching. *J. Res. Pers.* **40**, 99–120 (2006).
    DOI: 10.1016/j.jrp.2004.09.001
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jrp.2004.09.001
    Spanish–English bilinguals described their own personalities differently depending on the language of the questionnaire. In the **Discussion** it shows that language-dependent self-description happens in humans too.

## 7. Open science and Registered Reports (4)

42. Chambers, C. D. & Tzavella, L. The past, present and future of Registered Reports. *Nat. Hum. Behav.* **6**, 29–42 (2022).
    DOI: 10.1038/s41562-021-01193-7
    verified at: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-021-01193-7 (Nature's citation reads "Nat Hum Behav 6, 29–42 (2022)"; published online 15 November 2021, issue dated January 2022)
    Reviews the history and evidence for Registered Reports and gives guidance to authors and reviewers. It supports the choice of format in the **Methods/cover letter**.

43. Nosek, B. A., Ebersole, C. R., DeHaven, A. C. & Mellor, D. T. The preregistration revolution. *Proc. Natl Acad. Sci. USA* **115**, 2600–2606 (2018).
    DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1708274114
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1073/pnas.1708274114
    Makes the case for separating confirmatory from exploratory analyses through preregistration. In the **Methods** it frames the preregistered predictions and the labelling of exploratory analyses.

44. Scheel, A. M., Schijen, M. R. M. J. & Lakens, D. An excess of positive results: comparing the standard psychology literature with Registered Reports. *Adv. Methods Pract. Psychol. Sci.* **4**, 25152459211007467 (2021).
    DOI: 10.1177/25152459211007467
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/25152459211007467
    96% of hypotheses in standard psychology reports were supported, against 44% in Registered Reports. In the **Methods/cover letter** it explains why results reported through the RR route, null or unexpected ones included, are more credible.

45. Lakens, D., Scheel, A. M. & Isager, P. M. Equivalence testing for psychological research: a tutorial. *Adv. Methods Pract. Psychol. Sci.* **1**, 259–269 (2018).
    DOI: 10.1177/2515245918770963
    verified at: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/2515245918770963
    Explains how to test for the absence of a meaningful effect with the TOST procedure and a smallest effect size of interest. In the **Methods** it is needed if we claim Experience denial is "consistent" across languages, wordings and developers, which is a claim of equivalence and not merely a non-significant difference.
